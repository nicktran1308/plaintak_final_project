## 2. Gate evaluation

The gate decides whether a transformed span reaches the patient or falls back to the clinician's original text. Before this week it had twenty unit tests, all written against failures the author invented. Those tests establish that the gate catches what it was built to catch; they say nothing about its behaviour on model output it has never seen.

The evaluation ran in three rounds. Each produced a different kind of finding, and the sequence matters more than any single figure.

### 2.1 Method

Candidates were generated by running the simplifier over every content paragraph at all three ladder rungs, recording each output **together with the gate's verdict**. `simplify_once` could not be used, because it discards a blocked candidate and blocked candidates are precisely what the evaluation needs; the model was called directly and the gate applied separately.

**Annotation was blind.** The gate's decision was recorded at generation time and hidden during labelling. Displaying it would have anchored the annotator's judgement, and the resulting agreement would have measured nothing but that anchoring. The verdict was revealed only at scoring.

The annotator answered one question per candidate: did this rewrite lose, change, or invent any critical fact — a drug name, dose, frequency, date, duration, lab value, or an instruction not to do something?

### 2.2 Round one: 33 candidates

| | gate blocked | gate passed |
|---|---|---|
| **human: broken** | 2 | 1 |
| **human: sound** | 11 | 19 |

- Catch rate **66.7%**
- Precision **15.4%**
- False-positive rate **36.7%**

Eleven of thirteen blocks were wrong. Reading the individual cases, the false positives clustered into three causes rather than scattering:

| cluster | n | cause |
|---|---|---|
| `QHS` → "every night before bed" | 6 | a correct expansion of a clinical abbreviation, blocked because `qhs` was tagged as both a drug and a frequency and neither survived |
| "your kidneys **are not** working well" | 3 | the negation counter fired on descriptive negation, which states a condition rather than reversing an instruction |
| `Avoid` → "stay away from" | 1 | the negation vocabulary did not contain the lay paraphrase |
| `DOB` read as a drug | 1 | clinical NER tagged a patient-header abbreviation as a chemical |

Two of the three human-broken cases were the same metadata line, `DOB: 03/14/1955 (Age 71) Attending: R. Kaplan, MD`, which the intake step should never have sent to the simplifier. A date of birth is not an instruction. The effective sample of broken candidates was therefore **one**, and a catch rate computed on three items carries almost no information. It is reported here for completeness rather than as a result.

### 2.3 Fixes between rounds

Three vocabulary corrections and one scope correction, all identified by the blind annotation rather than by inspection:

**Bedtime vocabulary.** Six variants added (`every night`, `before bed`, `at night`, `each night`, `in the evening`, `hs`). This single cluster was 6 of 11 false positives.

**Descriptive negation excluded.** Only be- and have-forms are filtered, so *"your kidneys are not working well"* no longer counts as a prohibition while *"do not take"* still does. An earlier, broader filter also swallowed `do not`, which silently stopped the gate detecting real instruction reversals. That interim state was more dangerous than the false positives it fixed, and it is the reason the negation tests were re-run after every change.

**`without` removed from the negation markers.** *"Without interruption"* qualifies how a medication is taken rather than prohibiting it, and its presence blocked the correct rewrite *"keep taking … every night before bed"*. The with-food and empty-stomach distinctions it would otherwise have guarded are covered by verified glossary terms instead.

**Metadata paragraphs excluded from evaluation.** `intake.py` already classifies headers; the evaluation now also skips body lines beginning with patient, MRN, DOB, attending, admitted, or discharged.

### 2.4 Round two: 30 candidates

| | gate blocked | gate passed |
|---|---|---|
| **human: broken** | 0 | 0 |
| **human: sound** | 0 | 30 |

- False-positive rate **0.0%**, down from 36.7%
- Catch rate **undefined**

Perfect agreement, and a genuine improvement in precision. But the result carries a problem that must not be glossed over: **no natural fidelity failures occurred.** Across ten content paragraphs at three rungs each, Claude Haiku did not once drop, alter, or invent a critical fact.

That is a real finding about the model. It also means the gate's catch rate on naturally occurring failures is unmeasured, because there were no positives to catch. A detector cannot be evaluated on a corpus containing nothing to detect.

### 2.5 Round three: fault injection

To obtain a catch rate at all, known-sound output was corrupted in specific documented ways and the gate's detection measured. Every mutation type reproduces a failure reported in the literature this project cites.

| injected fault | n | caught | rate | reported by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| drop a dose | 18 | 18 | **100.0%** | Srinivasu et al. 2026 |
| alter a dose by an order of magnitude | 18 | 18 | **100.0%** | classic dosing harm |
| invent a dose not in the source | 12 | 12 | **100.0%** | Makhmutova et al. 2024 |
| drop a follow-up date | 6 | 6 | **100.0%** | — |
| drop a duration | 6 | 6 | **100.0%** | — |
| swap one drug for another | 15 | 14 | **93.3%** | cross-medication error |
| drop a frequency | 13 | 8 | 61.5% | Srinivasu et al. 2026 |
| flip a negation | 11 | 2 | **18.2%** | Kong et al. 2026 |
| round a number | 3 | 0 | **0.0%** | Picton et al. 2025 |
| **overall** | **102** | **84** | **82.4%** | |

**What this measures, and what it does not.** It measures sensitivity to known failure classes applied systematically to real model output. That is stronger evidence than hand-written unit tests, because the corpus was not selected to flatter the gate. It is **not** the catch rate on naturally occurring failures, which remains unknown because no natural failure occurred. Both facts belong in the final report and neither should be presented as the other.

### 2.6 The clinical NER layer earns its place

`swap_drug` detection was **0%** with named-entity recognition disabled and **93.3%** with it enabled. Substituting lisinopril for metformin leaves every number, date and frequency intact, so the regex core is structurally blind to it; only drug-name recognition catches it.

This settles a question left open in `week5_week8.md` §3.5, where the scispaCy layer was described as an enhancement over a regex core that "covers the important cases". It does not. Cross-medication substitution is a serious error class that the regex core cannot see at all.

## 3. Findings

Continuing the numbering from `week10_evaluation.md` §3.

### 3.1 Entity-set comparison verifies presence, not binding

This is the most important limitation the evaluation surfaced, and two independent measurements found it.

In round one, candidate `p15_r3` passed the gate with **every entity present** and zero reported reasons, yet was labelled broken on review:

> **original:** Avoid strenuous exertion for 7 days.
> **rewrite:** Do not do hard exercise. / Do not do heavy activity for 7 days.

One time-bounded instruction became two, and the first lost its bound. "Do not do hard exercise" now reads as permanent. The entity sets are identical because `duration:7d` is still present — it is simply **attached to the wrong clause**.

In round three, the same limitation produced the worst per-class result. Flipping a negation was detected only **18.2%** of the time, because the polarity check fires only when the output contains *zero* negations. Paragraph 13 reads *"Do not take NSAIDs. This means do not take ibuprofen. This means do not take naproxen."* Removing one prohibition leaves two, the count stays non-zero, and the corruption passes.

**Set comparison establishes that facts are present. It cannot establish what they govern.** Detecting the difference requires dependency parsing to determine each negation's and each modifier's scope. medspaCy's ConText module is the standard tool, and it is deliberately not used: installing it broke the pinned clinical NLP stack once already (`week9_integration.md` §3.1), and a working gate with a measured limitation is preferable to a broken environment.

This is reported as a stated limitation with a number attached, not as a defect awaiting a fix.

### 3.2 A detector cannot be evaluated on a corpus with nothing to detect

Round two produced a clean false-positive rate and an undefined catch rate simultaneously. The instinct to report "0% false positives" as the headline would be misleading, because the same run establishes nothing about whether the gate detects anything at all.

Fault injection is the standard resolution, and it is more defensible than the project's original unit tests for one specific reason: the mutations were applied **systematically to every applicable candidate** in a corpus generated before the mutations were designed. The unit tests, by contrast, were written against failures the author chose. Neither substitutes for natural failures, and the report says so.

### 3.3 Bare numerals are not tracked

Rounding `1.5 to 2 L` to `2 to 2 L` was detected **0 of 3 times**. A numeral only becomes an entity when a unit is attached to it, so the first value in a range is invisible to the gate.

Picton et al. (2025) identified inconsistent rounding as a specific GPT-4 failure mode, so this is a documented failure class the gate cannot currently see. Unlike the scope-binding limitation, this one is cheaply fixable by tracking bare numerals adjacent to unit-bearing entities.

### 3.4 Blind annotation changed the outcome

Every fix between rounds one and two came from reading individual mislabelled cases, not from inspecting the code. The `QHS` cluster in particular was invisible from the source: the vocabulary looked complete, and only seeing six consecutive blocks on a correct clinical expansion revealed the gap.

Had the annotation displayed the gate's verdict, the natural response to *"gate: BLOCK, missing drug:qhs"* would have been to accept it. The blind protocol is what made the false positives legible as false positives.

## 4. Interface

The patient-facing application is implemented in React and runs against the live backend.

**Screens.** Language selection, document intake, reading view with the simplification ladder and per-paragraph translation, and a printable clinician handoff sheet.

**The frontend never decides whether content is safe.** The gate returns a verdict and the scope classifier returns a response type; the components render what was already decided. `/api/simplify` returns the gate verdict as data so the client renders one of three states from a single response shape, and `/api/ask` returns `model_called` so the interface and the evaluation harness can both assert on the zero-cost refusal property rather than trust it.

**Accessibility, as implemented.** Body text at 20px and document text at 22px rather than the 16px default; a 48px minimum on every tappable target, above the 44px guideline; no hover-only controls and no session timeouts; the clinician's original English always on screen rather than behind a toggle; deferrals rendered in warm coral rather than red, because a refusal is the system working correctly and should not alarm the reader; `prefers-reduced-motion` respected.

**Typeface.** Be Vietnam Pro, chosen because it was drawn for Vietnamese diacritics. Most sans-serifs stack marks such as ế and ộ too tightly at large sizes, which is a legibility problem for precisely this reader.

### 4.1 Translation is on demand, not automatic

The proposal states that every screen appears in the patient's language from intake onward. The implementation translates interface text automatically but requires a press per paragraph for the document body.

Automatic translation of a full document would be roughly forty model calls and about a minute of waiting before the patient sees anything. On-demand translation is the pragmatic choice for a demonstrable system, and the divergence from the proposal is recorded here rather than left unstated. Answers to questions **are** returned in the patient's language automatically, so the language-first property holds for the conversational path.

## 6. Risks

**The gate's real-world catch rate is unmeasured and may remain so.** No natural failure occurred in 30 candidates. Generating more candidates on additional synthetic documents might produce some, but a corpus that yields no positives cannot be forced to yield them, and fault injection is the honest substitute rather than an equivalent.

**Negation scope is the weakest measured behaviour at 18.2%.** It is also the Kong et al. harm class. The mitigation is that critical prohibitions attached to medications are protected by glossary substitution during translation, so the exposure is largest for simplification of multi-prohibition paragraphs.

**Evaluation rests on a single document.** All gate results derive from one synthetic discharge instruction sheet. Its structure — numbered medication items, a diagnosis block, an ED-criteria list — is representative, but it is one document, and the generator that produced it is available to make more.
